# Course Description

Addresses concepts related to U.S. health services organization, delivery, financing, and quality assessment and improvement. Historical influences and trends, contemporary challenges, and strategies to address these challenges are examined.

The course is built around a single semester-long question: how does the U.S. health care system affect a particular health problem? You will answer it as a team, using systems thinking and a systems map that you build, expand, research, and revise across the term.

## What an Asynchronous Online Course Means Here

This course is fully online and asynchronous. We will not meet in person or on Zoom as a class. Your progress is self-paced within the weekly structure below. You review the assigned materials, work through each module with your team, and bring questions to me by email or in office hours.

# Course Student Learning Outcomes

The following are the approved learning objectives for the course. On successful completion, you will be able to:

1. Describe the organization and financing of the U.S. health care system.
2. Define and articulate significant challenges related to access, costs, and quality.
3. Describe approaches to health services organization and financing at different care levels.
4. Discuss ethical considerations in health care resource distribution and risk-benefit balance.
5. Apply concepts to real-world health care organizational and clinical settings.
6. Research and synthesize literature to develop recommendations for U.S. health care problems.
7. Synthesize and present research summaries and recommendations.
8. Critically evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of health care recommendations.

## Individual Assignments

### Exam 1: Systems Thinking

Exam 1 covers Module 2 and is taken in Canvas during the window of September 14-18, 2026. It consists of multiple-choice and multiple-answer questions and assesses your ability to reason about systems, not to memorize terms or definitions. You will analyze short system descriptions and, in some cases, simple diagrams. All necessary information is provided in each question.

The exam is fully auto-graded. You may move between questions and change your answers before submitting. You have 75 minutes. The time limit is set so that students who understand the material can finish comfortably without external resources.

This exam must be completed independently. AI tools may not be used. The structure and timing are designed so that external tools, shared answers, or AI-generated responses are unlikely to help, and may produce answers that are internally inconsistent or incorrect.

### Final Exam

The Final Exam is an individual assessment of your understanding of the organization and financing of the U.S. health care system, taken in Canvas during an open window in finals week, December 13-19, 2026. There is no single fixed date or time.

The exam is primarily multiple choice and focuses on explanation and reasoning rather than memorization. Questions ask you to interpret short scenarios and identify which explanations or outcomes are most consistent with how the U.S. health care system is structured, including financing, payment, governance, access, and equity. You are not expected to recall exhaustive factual detail. You are expected to connect system components, recognize constraints and tradeoffs, and explain why certain patterns or outcomes persist. Your team's final system map may be used to write part of your individualized final exam questions.

## Group Assignment: Systems Analysis of the U.S. Health Care System

Your team selects a U.S. health problem in Module 1 and analyzes how the health care system affects it, building and revising a systems map across Modules 3 through 7. Deliverables and due dates are in the table above.

Work in the project is evaluated using a single diagnostic question rather than a rubric:

> Does this work demonstrate the kind of systems thinking I expect at this stage of the project?

Work is graded at one of three levels: Yes (full credit), Partially (partial credit), or Not Yet (minimal credit). Grammar, formatting, and technical polish are not graded directly, but work must be intelligible. Where a deliverable includes a `.gv` file, it must render; a file that does not render cannot be evaluated and receives no credit for that component.

If technical problems prevent evaluation of your reasoning or system structure, the assignment may receive partial or minimal credit.

## Team Check-Ins

This course includes substantial team-based work. Team work supports learning; it is not a measure of effort, personality, or time spent. Contribution is understood as demonstrated understanding of the system being analyzed.

Individual contribution is evaluated on your ability to explain and engage with the system at the appropriate stage of the project. The course does not use peer grading, contribution logs, or time tracking. You will not be asked to evaluate or report on your teammates.

At two points in the term - Team Checkpoint 1 in Module 3 and Team Checkpoint 2 in Module 5 - you complete a brief individual check-in. These are private and are not shared with teammates. Check-ins are scored as a percentage of your team's score for that component.

| Check-in tier | Score, as a percentage of team score |
|---|---|
| Clear understanding | 100% |
| Partial understanding | 90% |
| Minimal understanding | 85% |
| Cannot explain the system | 75% |
| Non-submission | 0% |

Not submitting a check-in is scored separately from, and more harshly than, submitting a weak one. A check-in that shows you are struggling is worth engaging with the course about; it will never be scored as though you did not try.

If you appear to be struggling to understand or engage with the system, the default response is a learning-focused intervention, such as an individual systems memo. Escalation beyond that is rare. Group grades are not reduced because of individual difficulties.

## Non-Participation

This course has no attendance policy, but it does require participation. Nearly half of your grade depends on work produced with a team over the full term, and a student who disengages affects their teammates' ability to do the assignment. For that reason, non-participation is handled directly during the term rather than left to accumulate as missing scores at the end of it.

**What counts as non-participation.** I look at whether you are doing the work, not at how much time you spend or how your team divides tasks. Indicators include:

- Missing consecutive project deliverables or checkpoints
- Not submitting an individual check-in
- No visible contribution to your team's shared work product across a module
- Not responding to my emails about any of the above

**What happens.** If I see this pattern, you will receive two emails at your CSUF address:

1. First notice. I will tell you what I am seeing and ask you to reply. You have seven calendar days to respond.
2. Second and final notice. If I do not hear from you, I will send a second email stating that you must contact me within seven calendar days.

I will not send a third notice. If you do not respond to either one, what happens next depends on where in the term we are:

- On or before the withdrawal deadline of Friday, November 13, 2026, you can still withdraw from the course with a W, and my second notice will tell you so. A W is a better outcome for you than a WU.
- After the withdrawal deadline, withdrawal is no longer available. I will assign a WU at the end of the term, or an F if you have completed enough work for your performance to be evaluated.

Responding is a low bar, and it is the whole point. You do not need to have caught up, fixed anything, or explained yourself well. "I'm behind and I don't know where to start" is a sufficient reply and stops this process immediately. I would much rather know in Week 6 than discover it in Week 15, when almost nothing can be done.

This policy is not a penalty for struggling with the material, for being confused, or for producing weak work. Confusion and revision are expected parts of systems thinking. This policy addresses absence only: a student who has stopped participating and does not respond when contacted.

Notices are sent to your official CSUF email address. Checking that account is your responsibility.

# Policy on Generative Artificial Intelligence

This course permits the use of artificial intelligence and foundation models (for example ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Copilot, and similar tools) without restriction as part of your process. You are encouraged to use these tools for brainstorming, drafting, organizing ideas, checking clarity, exploring alternatives, and supporting learning.

All submitted work must ultimately represent your own reasoning, judgment, and understanding. You are responsible for the accuracy, integrity, and ethical quality of anything you submit, whether or not AI tools were used. AI systems are known to generate incorrect information, fabricated citations, biased outputs, and other errors. Reliance on AI output does not excuse mistakes or misrepresentation.

If you use AI tools, their role must be clearly acknowledged. Failure to acknowledge AI use is misrepresentation.

You may not submit work that is primarily or substantially generated by an AI system in place of your own thinking. Work is unacceptable if you cannot explain, defend, revise, or extend its reasoning independently of the tool. AI may support your work; it may not substitute for your analysis, synthesis, or conclusions.

The university's plagiarism policy continues to apply to uncited or improperly cited use of work created by other human beings. Misrepresenting AI-generated content as your own reasoning is a separate violation of academic integrity and is treated accordingly.

AI tools may not be used during examinations. Exams assess individual understanding and reasoning under constraint.

## AI Use Disclosure

If you used AI tools at any point in completing an assignment, include a brief disclosure at the end of your submission:

# Academic Integrity and Penalties for Academic Dishonesty

Most students hold themselves to high ethical standards. When others engage in academic misconduct, it reduces morale and diminishes the meaning of being a Titan. The following policy is intended to support everyone in the Titan community.

California State University has a strict policy against academic fraud and dishonesty. It is the student's responsibility to adhere to the guidelines regarding academic dishonesty set forth in the university handbook. Policies can be found at the [CSUF Dean of Students, Student Conduct policies and procedures](http://www.fullerton.edu/deanofstudents/conduct/policies-procedures.php). These include, but are not limited to, California Code of Regulations Title 5, Section 41301; Student Rights and Responsibilities (UPS 300.000); and the [Academic Dishonesty Policy (UPS 300.021)](https://www.fullerton.edu/senate/publications-policies-resolutions/ups/UPS%20300/UPS%20300.021.pdf).

The success of public health intervention relies on the public's trust in the integrity of public health workers. Every act of dishonesty undermines the potential for public health officials and their research to be trusted. Probity should be considered a requisite characteristic of all persons working in the health sciences and is expected of Titans.

## Penalties

This course includes both individual work and team work, so penalties are applied as follows.

- A student who inadvertently or negligently engages in academic dishonesty will be ineligible to receive credit for the related work - that is, a zero on that assignment.
- The student may be reassessed on all course material using a variety of modalities, including oral examination.
- Evidence of academic dishonesty will be reported to the Dean of Students and the CSUF Student Conduct team.
- The student will receive an F in the course upon a determination that the incident was the result of willful intent or gross negligence.
- Submitting work generated by generative AI or a large language model, in violation of the policy above, is academic dishonesty and is subject to these penalties.

On team deliverables, penalties apply to the individual student responsible for the dishonest content. A team is not penalized for the conduct of one member, and I will ask team members individually to explain the work in question in order to establish responsibility. Where every member of a team knowingly submitted dishonest work, the penalties apply to each of them individually.
