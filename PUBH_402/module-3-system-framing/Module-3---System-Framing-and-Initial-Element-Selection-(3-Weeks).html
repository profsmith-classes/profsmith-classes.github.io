Module 3 - System Framing and Initial Map (3 Weeks)

NoteA quick reminder before you start

This is often the point in the course where uncertainty increases. That’s expected.

If you feel stuck or unsure:

  • revisit the Welcome page.
  • check the Class Tips page, Tips for Working in This Course
  • and remember that confusion + engagement is normal here.

You do not need a fully formed question to reach out.

Module Orientation

In this module, you will move from forming your team and a preliminary exploration of your problem to the beginning of the systems analysis and of an understanding of the US health care system.

The goal of this module is not to finalize your work, but to begin your analysis.

This module is the first module where the direction of learning shifts from my guidance to you and your team. As you begin this work, check-in with how you are feeling. Feeling unsure, a little confused, and maybe a bit of anxiety is normal at this stage!

What You Should Be Able to Do After This Module

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

  1. Describe an initial system framing, i.e. what outcomes or markers will you rely on as you create you begin your analysis.
  2. Select key elements from a list provided to you and explain your reasoning by the your choice of elements.
  3. Create a first draft of your system map by creating a .gv file.

These describe ways of thinking, not tasks to complete.

Core Concepts and Materials

Your team’s goal here is to create the first draft of your systems analysis. If you need to, review 2.1 Systems Thinking in Module 2 - Systems Thinking Foundations as this module will rely on those concepts.

System Framing

Think about your problem and as a team outline some boundaries for your work. What topics might you include and what topics would be outside your system? What measures will you look at to explore the problem? What is the outcome you would like to see if this problem were solved

Element Selection

You are provided with a structured list of system elements representing major domains of the U.S. health care and public health system, including:

  • care providers
  • federal and state policy / enforcement and accountability
  • financing / insurance
  • funding
  • payment models
  • pharmaceuticals and devices
  • public health
  • structural determinants
  • structural inequities
  • workforce / infrastructure

This list is a shared analytical starting point, not a complete description of the system.

If it helps to see every element already formatted as Graphviz syntax before you start building your own file, download the System Elements Template — it lists every element as a plain node with its full definition as a comment. It has no edges or loops; it’s a reference, not a systems map.

Your first task is to explore these elements as a team through discussion, research, and sharing. Your goal is to begin to understand which elements are important for understanding your health problem and which elements are less important. Check the Research Resources for some tips and guidance on research if you need a refresher.

ImportantBe Specific!

Your discussion of elements must be specific and related to the health problem that your team is working on.

You should start with your assigned text book and then being to explore additional sources for more information. Your sources must be reliable and relevant.1

Your team’s goal is to develop a general sense or understanding of how these elements work together to affect your health problem.

Select 12 system elements from the provided list of elements that your team believes are most important for explaining how the system produces outcomes related to your health problem.

Your selection must include at least: - one Structural Determinant - one Structural Inequity - one Financing or Insurance element - one Payment element - one Federal and State Policy or Enforcement and Accountability element - one Workforce or Infrastructure element

You are not limited to the provided list. If your team’s research surfaces an element that isn’t on the list but is clearly important for your health problem, you may add it — but additional elements do not count toward the category minimums above. An added element needs its own good structural reason for being in your map; it cannot substitute for, say, the required Structural Determinant.

WarningKeep track of research activities

You will be asked in Module 5 - Research and System Refinement (3 Weeks) to reflect on your work and efforts in creating your system map. You will also be asked to share the sources you used in developing your map and materials.

Make a decision now as a team how you will keep track of your conversations and efforts as well as how you will save and maintain a record of sources that you used in your efforts!

Draft System Map

With your element list, you will write your first .gv file for use with Graphviz. This file will layout the relationships between all your elements.

Go through the DOT Explainer to learn how to create a .gv file for use with Graphviz.

As you connect the elements, include polarity and delays (if any). As you build your structure, if an element has no connection to another element. You will simply list the element.

Once you are ready, use an online Graphviz tool to see your map.

Before you move to Module 4

Module 3 is not finished when you have 12 elements and a .gv file that lists them. It’s finished when your team has worked through, together, one provisional feedback loop for each member of your team — a closed causal path where an effect eventually feeds back on its own cause. You do not need these to be correct or final. You do need one for every teammate, because in Module 4 each team member will take individual ownership of a loop, and loop ownership can only be assigned to loops that already exist. If your team has four members, you should leave Module 3 with four identifiable loops, not one. If your team reaches the end of Module 3 with a list of connected elements but fewer loops than teammates, treat that as important information about your system — not as a formatting problem to fix later.

How This Module Connects to Your System Map

This module is focused on creating your first systems map and is the basis for your analysis moving forward.

Module Deliverables

1. Element List

What to submit

Following the guidance above, identify your 12 elements. Make sure that you include: - one Structural Determinant - one Structural Inequity - one Financing or Insurance element - one Payment element - one Federal and State Policy or Enforcement and Accountability element - one Workforce or Infrastructure element

For each selected element, provide: - the element name - the category it belongs to - one sentence explaining why this element matters for your system, i.e. how does it affect another of your chosen elements?2

For the elements in an area that you did not select, provide: - the element name - The category it belongs to - and one sentence explaining why your team did not choose this element.

Format:

Word document or PDF with 1” margins all around, Times New Roman, 10pt, 1.5 spacing. Use the template provided on Canvas attached to the assignment.

Submission location

  • Canvas - Module 3 Element List

2. Graphviz .gv file

Use the Systems Map Template as the basis for your team’s .gv file. Your file must:

  • Include the name of your health problem.
  • Twelve selected element from your element list
  • Relationships between elements written as edges, labeled with polarity, and delays (if applicable)
  • Feedback loops are indentified
  • Comments on elements and relationships included in the file.
  • Must be a valid .gv file that will render as a graph. (Use an online tool to check this); upload a copy of your diagram that you produced.

3. Team Checkpoint 1 (Individual Check-In)

This is an individual, private submission — not a team deliverable. It is graded for completion/understanding and is not shared with your teammates. It tests whether you were personally present for your team’s work in this module, not just whether your team’s file looks complete.

  1. Name three elements from your team’s .gv file and, in your own words (not the definition from the System Elements list), explain what each one represents in your specific health problem.
  2. Your team should have identified one provisional feedback loop for each team member. Pick one of those loops and trace it step by step: which elements are in it, in what order, and where does the effect loop back to its starting cause? If your team has identified fewer loops than teammates, say so directly and describe what you’ve tried so far — that answer is not penalized as a wrong answer, it’s the honest state of your work.
  3. Pick one connection (edge) in your loop and explain why it’s labeled the polarity it is (same-direction vs. opposite-direction) — what would have to be true about the real world for that polarity to hold?
  4. What is one thing about your system that you personally find uncertain, surprising, or that you and a teammate see differently? You don’t need to have resolved it.

Format and submission

Canvas file submission (Word or PDF), one file per student. Half a page to one page, single-spaced — this is a cap, not a target; specificity matters more than length. Label your file with your name and team.

How this is scored

Tier Score (% of team score)
Clear understanding 100%
Partial understanding 90%
Minimal understanding 85%
Cannot explain the system 75%
Non-submission 0%

Not submitting is scored separately from, and more harshly than, submitting a weak answer.

How This Will Be Evaluated

You are defining the boundaries of your system and making your initial structural choices explicit.

Each component below is graded separately: the Element List is worth 12 points and the Initial System Map is worth 8 points, for 20 points total. This evaluates the quality of your reasoning and the boundary-setting choices you made — not polish, formatting, or grammar. Work must be intelligible, but technical polish is not the target at this stage.

1. Element List (12 points)

Does this element list demonstrate defensible, problem-specific boundary-setting — not just a mechanically complete list?

Evaluation Level Points
Meets Requirements 12
Partially Meets Requirements 8
Does Not Meet Requirements 6

Meets Requirements — 12 points

  • All 12 elements are selected and category minimums are satisfied.
  • Each selected element’s rationale does both: (a) describes an actual relationship to another chosen element — not a restatement of the element’s definition — and (b) explains why the element matters specifically for your health problem, not health care in general.
  • Test: if a rationale could be dropped into another team’s element list, for a different health problem, with only the element name changed, it does not meet this standard.
  • Excluded-category rationales hold to the same test — a generic “not relevant to our topic” does not meet it either.

Partially Meets Requirements — 8 points

  • Mechanically complete, but rationales satisfy only one side of the test above — e.g., ties the element to another element structurally but never connects it to your team’s specific problem, or asserts problem-relevance without any structural relationship.
  • Or several rationales are thin, generic, or interchangeable across elements.

Does Not Meet Requirements — 6 points

  • Mechanically incomplete (fewer than 12 elements, missing a required category).
  • Or rationales are largely absent, copy-pasted, or generic enough to apply to any health problem.

2. Initial System Map (8 points)

Does this map render correctly, contain everything required, and show a genuine first encounter with feedback in the system?

Evaluation Level Points
Meets Requirements 8
Partially Meets Requirements 6
Does Not Meet Requirements 4

Meets Requirements — 8 points

  • The .gv file renders correctly and includes your health problem’s name, all twelve elements, edges labeled with polarity (and delays where used), and comments that make your reasoning legible to an outside reader.
  • Your team has traced one complete, closed provisional feedback loop for each team member: for every loop, every element in it, every connecting edge, and the point where the effect returns to its starting cause are identifiable in the file or its comments.

Partially Meets Requirements — 6 points

  • The map renders and includes most required content, but polarity or delay labeling is inconsistent or asserted without a supporting comment.
  • Or one or more claimed loops don’t actually close, or your account of a loop attempt is too vague to verify.
  • Or your team has fewer complete, closed loops than it has team members (but at least one), with a genuinely documented attempt to find the rest.
  • Or your team genuinely attempted the loop-finding process for every member and documented those attempts honestly, but a loop failed to emerge for one or more members — see Before you move to Module 4.

Does Not Meet Requirements — 4 points

  • The file does not render, or is missing required content entirely.
  • Or no loop is identified for anyone and there’s no evidence of a genuine attempt.
